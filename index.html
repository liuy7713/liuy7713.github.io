About

I trained in mathematics and computer science at École Polytechnique, with an exchange term at King’s College London. Over those three years my interest moved from the theory itself towards applying it to clinical and population health data.

I am now reading for the MSc in Health Data Analytics and Machine Learning at Imperial’s School of Public Health, supported by an SPH Masters Scholarship. Alongside the degree I am a visiting researcher in the EBS Omics group, where my bachelor thesis on GP prescription records has developed into an ongoing project.

My first clinical experience was a summer at Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital in 2023, observing plastic and reconstructive surgery. In 2025 I joined the research summer school at St Thomas’ Hospital, building software for X-ray quality assurance.

Prescription‑Mediated Multi‑Morbidity Index

Multimorbidity is usually measured by counting diagnoses, as established indices such as Charlson and Elixhauser do. PMMI is built from GP prescription records instead. The question is whether the medicines a person takes carry information about their mortality risk that their diagnoses miss.

  1. PrescriptionsGP prescription records in UK Biobank, grouped into sex-specific British National Formulary categories
  2. SelectionElastic-net Cox regression and stability selection calibrated with SHARP, to find a small set that holds up when the data change
  3. CheckRandom survival forests, to see what a non-linear model would pick
  4. ScoreA short integer score you can read off without a model

Developed from my bachelor thesis. Supervised by Dr Dragana Vuckovic and Prof. Marc Chadeau-Hyam, with Dr Rin Wada. EBS Omics group, Department of Epidemiology & Biostatistics, School of Public Health.

Finished King’s College London, BMEIS · summer 2025

Checking X-ray image quality automatically

Hospitals check their fluoroscopy systems by imaging a test object, a phantom, and finding the finest pattern of lines the system can still resolve. This is usually done by eye, which is slow and varies between readers.

At the research summer school at St Thomas’ Hospital I wrote a MATLAB pipeline that automates this reading for the Leeds TOR 18FG phantom. It takes about a second per image and resolves detail roughly 50% finer than human readers. Results were validated against ImageJ.

  1. PrepareClean up the DICOM image
  2. LocateFind the phantom’s rim with Canny edges and a Hough transform
  3. AlignStraighten it with Powell optimisation and a Radon transform
  4. MeasureContrast in each of the 21 bar groups, cut off at MTF10
  5. ReportThe limiting resolution, in line pairs per mm

Supervised by Prof. Kawal Rhode, School of Biomedical Engineering & Imaging Sciences, King’s College London. With thanks to Carlo Saija for his support with MATLAB.
